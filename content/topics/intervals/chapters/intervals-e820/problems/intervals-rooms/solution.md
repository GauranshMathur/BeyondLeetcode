Sort the intervals by `lo` (stable, so equal `lo` keep the order they were added). Keep a heap `busy` of `(hi, room)`, soonest end first, a heap `free` of room numbers, lowest first, and `next_room = 1`. For each interval `lo hi` in that order: while the top of `busy` has `hi' < lo`, pop it and push its room on `free`. Take the room from `free` if there is one, else take `next_room` and add 1 to it. Record the room for this interval's position in the input order, and push `(hi, room)` on `busy`. Print the rooms in input order, or `none`. The heaps come from Python's [`heapq`](https://docs.python.org/3.13/library/heapq.html) and Go's [`container/heap`](https://github.com/golang/go/blob/a10e42f219abb9c5bc4e7d86d9464700a42c7d57/src/container/heap/heap.go#L5-L7), and the TypeScript Reference Code writes a small one.

**Why it is right.** A room is only ever in one of the two heaps. A meeting is moved to `free` once its end is before the current `lo`, and since `lo` only grows along the sorted order, it stays free for every later meeting until it is used. So `free` holds exactly the rooms that can take the current meeting, and taking the lowest follows the rule. A new room is opened only when every room in use ends at or after `lo`, which is when the number of meetings that hold `lo` equals the number of rooms. So the highest room number used equals what `depth` prints.

**Cost.** Sorting is O(n log n) in the **worst case**. Each interval is pushed on `busy` once and popped at most once, and its room goes through `free` at most once each time it is released, so there are O(n) heap operations at O(log n) each in the **worst case**. In all, one `rooms` is O(n log n) in the **worst case**, with O(n) extra space.
