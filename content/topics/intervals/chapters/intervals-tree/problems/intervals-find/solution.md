Keep every interval that `add` and `place` put in, in a list sorted by `lo`, equal `lo` in the order they were added (a stable sort). Treat the list as a balanced tree with no pointers: the middle item of any part is its root, the halves on each side are its children. Store at each middle item the largest `hi` in its part. To answer `find lo hi`, visit a part like this: if its largest `hi` is below `lo`, skip it. Otherwise visit the left half, then look at the middle item, which is picked when its `lo` is at most `hi` and its `hi` is at least `lo`. Visit the right half only if the middle item's `lo` is at most `hi`, because everything to the right starts at least as late. After `add` or `place`, rebuild the list and the stored numbers before the next `find`.

**Why it is right.** An interval overlaps the query exactly when its `lo` is at most the query's `hi` and its `hi` is at least the query's `lo`. A part with largest `hi` below `lo` has no interval satisfying the second condition. In the right half of a middle item that starts after the query ends, every `lo` is at least as large, so none satisfies the first. Visiting left, middle, right gives the picked intervals in order of `lo`, and the stable sort keeps the order of equal `lo`.

**Cost.** Rebuilding is O(n log n) in the **worst case** (the sort). A search is O((k + 1) log n) in the **worst case** when it picks k intervals, because a part that is entered either holds a pick or is on one path down the tree (this is the Chapter's own analysis). A plain scan of the list is O(n) per query and passes the Tests as well, but it is the work the tree exists to avoid.
